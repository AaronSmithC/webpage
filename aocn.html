Aegis Orbital Compute Node (AOCN)

A 1 MW batch-compute node in a dawn-dusk orbit: power without a grid, heat without water, and a break-even stated in the open

Rev C — November 2026. A correction and its consequences. Rev B said the 650 km dawn-dusk orbit has no eclipse. It has a shadow season: 89 days a year, up to 19.6 minutes an orbit. Rev C keeps the orbit and carries the full load through the shadow on a 1,182 kWh battery. The array grows from 5,400 to 7,630 m², the node from ~65 to ~86 t, and the break-even launch price falls from about $300 to about $230 per kilogram. The coolant is chosen: glycol-water on the trays and silicone oil in the wings, in two loops through a heat exchanger, so that the wings can go cold without anything freezing. Throttling, a higher orbit with no shadow, and a low-inclination orbit were each priced and are shown in the orbit section. The radiation figures now come from a trapped-proton model instead of a rule of thumb. The Rev B radiator sizing stands.

What It Is

The Aegis Orbital Compute Node (AOCN) is a free-flying compute unit: one megawatt of commercial accelerators in a shielded vault, a fixed solar sheet that faces the sun all year, a battery for the weeks when the Earth gets in the way, and pumped-loop radiators that never see the sun. It runs batch work: model training, large-scale inference, simulation, and the processing of data that is already in orbit. It does not run a cloud. Nothing on it answers a request in milliseconds, and nothing on it needs to.

The node is the unit of scale. Capacity grows by flying more of them, not by making one bigger. Every node is sized, launched, and serviced the same way, and the compute inside is expected to be replaced every three to five years while the power and thermal plant stays.

Why Put Compute in Orbit

The honest case is narrower than the slogans and stronger than the sceptics allow. Three things are true at once.

1. The constraint on Earth is the grid, not the chip

Terrestrial AI capacity is gated by interconnect queues measured in years, by water for cooling, and by siting permits. In orbit none of those exist. Power is not free; it costs launched array mass. But a square metre of array in a dawn-dusk orbit produces more than four times the energy of the same square metre on the ground, because it is lit about 96 % of the year at the full solar constant and needs storage for twenty minutes at a time, not for a night. The question is whether that buys back the launch cost, and the economics section answers it with a number rather than a mood.

2. Batch work does not need a fast link to Earth

Training a large model is weeks of computation on a dataset that arrives once and a result that leaves once. That is the opposite of a cloud workload. The dataset goes up on launched storage: ten petabytes on enterprise SSDs is under 200 drives and well under half a tonne. Checkpoints and weights come down over optical crosslinks. A dawn-dusk orbit passes a polar ground station on nearly every revolution, so downlink capacity is not the limit. The data section sizes it.

3. Some customers are already in space

Earth-observation constellations downlink raw data they could process in orbit. Cislunar operations, when they exist, will want compute that is seconds away rather than a round trip through Earth. This is the only case with no terrestrial competitor. It is also the smallest market today, which is why AOCN is not designed around it. It is designed so that the same node serves it when it arrives.

What AOCN is not. It is not a cloud region, not an edge for latency-sensitive traffic, and not a claim that orbital compute is cheaper today. At current launch prices the plant around the accelerators costs two to six times what a terrestrial hall and its power do. The page states where that crosses over.

Orbit Selection

A dawn-dusk sun-synchronous orbit keeps its plane at the day-night terminator all year, so a flat array in that plane faces the sun without moving. Rev B chose it at 650 km believing it had no eclipse at any season. That was wrong, and the error is worth showing. A sun-synchronous plane at 650 km is inclined 98°, so its normal lies 8° off the equator. Add the 23.4° tilt of the Earth's axis and the sun reaches 31.4° off the orbit normal at one solstice; the Earth begins to cover it at 24.8°. Rev B counted the 23.4° and left out the 8°.

The result is a shadow season of 89 days a year, with up to 19.6 minutes of shadow in each 97.6-minute orbit at its middle. For the other 276 days the node is sunlit all the way round. The array's worst day is the same day, and it falls near aphelion. No dawn-dusk orbit below about 1,400 km escapes this, because the inclination that keeps the plane sun-synchronous climbs with altitude.

Rev B set out to delete the battery because everything downstream gets simpler without one. Most of that survives. The array is still a fixed sheet, the sun is still constant, the radiators still sit in the array's shade. Nothing moves. For 276 days nothing cycles; for 89 the battery does, and because it carries the full load, nothing else does.

  • Shadow season. Centred on the June solstice for an 18:00 ascending node, on December for 06:00. The battery section sizes it.
  • Fixed geometry. The array is a flat sheet in the orbit plane facing the sun; it needs no gimbal. Radiators lie in the plane containing the sun vector and nadir, so their faces look along the velocity vector, never at the sun and only obliquely at Earth. The array shades them as well.
  • Ground contact. Both poles every orbit, which is what makes the optical downlink budget work.
  • Drag. The array is edge-on to the velocity vector; the radiators are not. About 2,100 m² of ram area at 650 km costs ~13 m/s per year, about 70 kg of xenon per year on electric propulsion. Atomic-oxygen-tolerant radiator coatings are required. The same drag brings a dead node down, which the higher orbit could not promise.
  • Radiation. Behind the vault wall the dose is trapped protons from the South Atlantic Anomaly, about 150 rad a year. The electrons of the polar horns stop in the wall. The vault section carries it.

Thermal: Radiator Sizing

At steady state every watt of electrical input becomes heat. Commercial accelerators hold junction temperatures below about 363 K; direct liquid cooling delivers coolant at 305–320 K and returns it 10–15 K warmer. The radiator therefore runs between roughly 330 K inlet and 305 K outlet, a 315 K mean radiating temperature. Rev 1.1 stated 290–330 K; the same range, now pinned to a sizing point.

Two-sided rejection follows the Stefan-Boltzmann law with an emissivity of 0.88. An effective factor of 0.6 covers Earth infrared on the oblique face, view-factor losses between wings, coating degradation, and fin efficiency. With the array shading the wings and no direct solar load, this factor is conservative.

The node rejects 1.2 MW, so the baseline radiator area is ~2,000 m² two-sided, carried as two 50 × 20 m wings with isolable sections. At 6 kg/m² for deployable pumped-loop panels the wings weigh about 12 t. If a future accelerator generation tolerates hotter coolant, the same wings reject more; the plant does not need to change when the trays do.

Two loops

Rev B said "a pumped single-phase loop" and named no fluid. The fluid decides more than it seems. The wings that reject 1.2 MW keep rejecting when the load comes off, and settle near 205 K; the water-glycol that commercial liquid-cooled trays are built for freezes at 223 K. One loop of it freezes in the wings within two hours of the load going. One loop of a fluid that tolerates the cold costs the megawatt, because cold plates made for water-glycol carry half the heat with it.

Rev C carries two loops: water-glycol in the vault, through the trays, and silicone oil in the wings, which does not freeze above 162 K, with a counterflow heat exchanger between them. Each loop has redundant pumps. The exchanger costs 5 K, so the trays' coolant runs that much warmer than the wings', about 2 t, and about 9 kW more pumping. It buys three things: commercial trays on the fluid they are made for; a punctured wing that cannot drain the vault; and wings that can be left at the temperature of the sky for as long as the load is off or the sun is lost.

What a model of the loops adds

A time-stepping model of the trays, both loops and the wings by section (November 2026) reproduces this sizing point and shows where it is thin. Its tray, coolant and overhead figures are first assumptions, so these are directions, not specifications.

  • Margin. With all eight wing sections the loops carry about 1,020 kW of accelerator before the junctions reach their limit; with seven, about 930 kW. A punctured section is isolated in seconds, with about a kilogram of oil lost, and costs 7 % of the load, not the node.
  • Pumps. With no flow in either loop the trays warm about 8 K a minute and reach the junction limit in under a minute. Changing to a standby pump takes seconds and the load does not notice. With both pumps of a loop gone the trays are switched off inside eight seconds.
  • The cold case. With the load off, the wing pumps slow until the vault's coolant holds at 300 K and then barely turn. The wings go to 200 K and stay there for a day with nothing frozen, and take the megawatt straight back. With the sun lost for a day the vault still holds, on a third of the battery. The one thing that must not happen is cold oil pumped hard through the exchanger against a cold vault, which freezes its water side inside three hours; holding the vault's temperature with the wing pumps is what prevents it.

The array has to make 1.2 MW on its last day, at its worst sun angle, through a blanket that is not all cells. Compute Vault and Radiation

Rev 1.1 carried 15–50 t of water and structure as shielding for a compute payload. That is a crew figure applied to electronics. Silicon does not need a storm shelter; it needs a tolerable total dose, a handled upset rate, and a refresh plan.

  • What flies. The accelerators, memory, processors, network and storage are commercial parts, screened and proton-tested but not hardened. Nothing hardened comes within orders of magnitude of them, and the economics on this page are those of commercial hardware. Everything that keeps the node alive is the opposite: the flight computer, the tray power switches that clear a latch-up, the bus converters and the pump and valve controllers are space-grade.
  • Total dose. Behind a vault wall of 10–15 mm aluminium-equivalent the dose is about 150 rad a year, nearly all of it protons from the South Atlantic Anomaly (AP8 model at solar minimum; protons only, good to about a factor of two). The wall stops the electrons of the polar horns outright but takes only about a third off the protons between 5 and 15 mm, so it is not thickened further. A tray is planned to 2 krad, where commercial memory can begin to misbehave, well short of the 10–30 krad at which parts fail. Five years is about 0.75 krad. The 3–5 year tray life is set by the commercial hardware cycle, not by the dose.
  • Why not higher. The same wall at 1,600 km sees about 5,400 rad a year, and the node would spend half of every orbit in protons above 30 MeV against 8 % here. That is what ruled out the orbit with no shadow.
  • Upsets. Polar and SAA passes raise the single-event rate on unhardened parts. The answer is architectural: ECC memory, frequent checkpointing to the local storage pool, and scheduling that expects a few percent of throughput lost to restarts. This is the open engineering question on the node and the page does not pretend it is closed.
  • Geometry. The vault is an unpressurised cylinder, ⌀4 × 10 m, with cold-plated trays on rails so a robotic servicer can swap a tray without breaking the coolant loop. Storage, power conversion, and the loop pumps live in the same shell. Vault structure and shielding together are ~5 t, not 15–50.
  • Serviceability. Interfaces are EVA-compatible so a crewed servicing mission is possible, but nothing on the node requires a person. The array blankets and radiator wings are line-replaceable units on the same truss standard.

Economics: Where It Crosses Over

Both an orbital node and a terrestrial hall buy the same accelerators, and those dominate the bill either way: on the order of $40–50 M per megawatt of current-generation hardware. What differs is the plant around them. A terrestrial AI hall costs roughly $10–15 M per MW to build, plus around $0.6–0.9 M per MW-year in energy. Over a five-year hardware life that is about $15–20 M per MW of plant and power, before the years spent waiting for an interconnect.

The orbital node replaces all of that with launch mass. At 86 t per megawatt:

The crossover sits near $230 per kilogram to sun-synchronous orbit, before counting on-orbit assembly, qualification, and the operations team, which push it lower, and before counting the value of skipping a multi-year grid queue, which pushes it higher. That price does not exist today. It is the stated goal of fully reusable heavy launch, and the point of writing the number down is that it is a launch-price gate, not a technology gate. Everything else on this page is buildable now.

Rev B put the crossover at $300. Most of the difference is not the price of a decision; it is the price of the correction. The $300 node could not hold its load for 89 days of the year, and its coolant had no name.

Refresh. Each accelerator generation relaunches ~12 t of trays and storage per node. At $500 /kg that is $6 M against $40 M or more of hardware inside them, so refresh cost is not what decides the case.

Scaling

Nothing in the node gets easier when it gets bigger. Deployed area, bending modes, pointing disturbance, fault-domain size, and the mass of any single failure all grow faster than the compute they carry. A 1 MW node is already a 109 m sheet with 50 m wings. The unit is fixed and the fleet grows.

  • Plane fill. Nodes in the same dawn-dusk plane share ground stations and can crosslink for distributed training. Ten nodes is 10 MW of accelerator in one plane with one ground segment.
  • Independent failure. A punctured loop or a lost wing takes out one node, throttled, not a fleet.
  • Same servicer. One robotic servicing vehicle and one tray standard cover every node in the plane.
  • Lifecycle split. Plant lasts 10–15 years; trays turn over in 3–5. The plant is the asset; the trays are inventory.

Beyond Earth Orbit

The node does not care which body it orbits, but the case does. Around the Moon or Mars there is no advantage until there are customers there, and the Earth link gets worse. Where the case changes is the lunar surface, and only one part of it.

The lunar surface as a heat sink, with the caveat

The daytime surface is not a cold sink. At the equator it reaches ~390 K at noon for two weeks, and regolith is an insulator, so nothing conducts into the ground worth counting. A radiator on the day side of the Moon looks at hot ground and a hot sun for 14 days and then at nothing but darkness for 14 more, with no power.

The polar surface is different. Rim sites near the south pole are lit 80–90 % of the year at grazing angles, and next to them are permanently shadowed floors at 40–100 K. A vertical radiator on a rim, facing the shadowed floor and the cold sky, rejects about 490 W/m² one-sided at 315 K with no Earth infrared and no solar load. Per square metre that is comparable to the orbital wing. The difference is everything around it: no launch of the plant from Earth once regolith structure and local water are available, no deployment mechanisms, radiators that can be built as walls, and the program's own water on site for thermal storage across the short polar nights.

The LEO node is the near-term product and the testbed for the plant. The lunar polar node is where the plant stops being launched. That is the same gate the rest of the program is waiting on: a viable moonbase and industrial-scale lunar water.
