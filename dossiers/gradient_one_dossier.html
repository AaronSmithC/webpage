Aegis Station

Gradient One

Three-Arm Multi-Gravity Validation Platform, 1.6 rpm
Design Dossier  |  Rev B — September 2026  |  Not for Hardware Commitment

1. Purpose

Gradient One (G1) is a rotating orbital research platform that places Earth gravity, Mars gravity, lunar gravity and a rotating microgravity hub in one vehicle at one spin rate, for six-month crewed campaigns. It is the platform defined to close the Artificial Gravity Requirements Evaluation (AGRE) and to retire the rotating-structure risks that Aegis Station carries as assumptions: 1g at 1.6 rpm for months, the hub/rotor boundary under rotation, radial fluids at full head, balance authority, and spin transients with crew aboard.

Two questions, one vehicle. The station's question is whether people can live and work for months at 1g produced by rotation at 1.6 rpm. The surface program's question is how bone, cardiovascular, ocular and plant endpoints respond to lunar and Mars gravity over months, against a 1g control in the same vehicle. Both need several gravity levels, held for a long time, in one place, with rotation present.

Rev B in one paragraph. The single-arm truss with a counterweight becomes a self-balancing three-arm rotor that spins about its major axis. The despun hub becomes a small docking node joined to a rotating 0g hub by a cyclic transfer lock, contactless power and an optical data link, with no continuous rotary seal in any life-critical path. Campaigns move from 30–90 days to 180 days, crew from 2–4 to 10, because the requirements ask for six-month exposures. Mass is bottom-up at about 227 t. The cost class moves from $0.6–1.4 B to $5–10 B to first campaign.

2. Geometry and gravity bands

Spin rate is fixed by the 1g radius: ω = √(g/R) = 0.167 rad/s = 1.60 rpm at R = 350 m. Band radii follow from a = ω²r. Each module is mounted radially with three decks at 2.4 m pitch; the band radius is the middle deck.

3. Configuration

A Y-shaped rotor: three open-truss arms at 120°, each about 344 m from hub port to tip, with an identical habitat module hung from each tip at the 1g radius. The lunar module sits inboard on arm A and the Mars module inboard on arm B. A rotating hub module joins the arms and houses the 0g crew. A despun node on the spin axis carries three docking ports, six deployable arrays on two gimbals, reboost thrusters and antennas.

Why three arms

A planar rotor with three or more equal arms has spin-axis inertia twice its transverse inertia, so it spins about its major axis and energy dissipation from crew, fluids and flexure drives it toward the spin it already has. A two-arm dumbbell has equal spin and in-plane transverse inertia and no such preference. Three identical tip modules also balance in the plane without a dead counterweight, which removes the Rev A counterweight and its ambiguity.

Why 350 m and 1.6 rpm

Earth is the 1g control for the biology; the radius is chosen because 1g at 1.6 rpm is the station's operating point and AGRE reports tolerance per spin rate. The sizing model quantifies the alternative:

4. Modules

One common pressurized module, five copies: 4.5 m diameter, 11 m hull, three 2.4 m decks and a utility deck, about 14 t dry and 17 t outfitted, in the class of the ISS laboratory and node modules. Every module is mounted radially, long axis along the arm, so the centripetal load runs through the hull along the axis its launch structure already carries at several g. Tip modules hang from the arm end; the Moon and Mars modules sit beside the arm on cradles carrying 0.17 g and 0.38 g of their mass, 28 kN and 63 kN respectively. The hub-facing end of every module carries the port that the transit car docks to.

5. Hub/rotor boundary

Rev B adopts the principle of the station's hub/ring boundary: no continuous rotary mechanical interface carries a life-critical flow. Every crossing is cyclic, contactless, or eliminated by distributing the function to both sides.

The vehicle is a dual-spin spacecraft: a rotor spinning about its major axis and a despun platform on the same bearing, an arrangement flown for decades on communications satellites at far higher rates. A seized bearing turns the node into a co-rotating hub; the two crew vehicles that remain docked for the whole campaign are the crew's way home in either case. The boundary's performance under rotation is a campaign objective (AGRE-F-012) at the station's spin rate.

Attitude

The spin axis is held near the orbit normal so that the reboost direction stays in the plane the despun node can point in. For a flat rotor the orbit-averaged gravity-gradient torque precesses the spin axis about the orbit normal at Ω = ¾ n² cos θ / ω, about 28° per day at 450 km. The orbit normal itself regresses at 4.9° per day at 51.6°. The axis therefore follows the orbit with a lag of order 10°, without propellant. The coupled motion, including the despun node's pointing and array shadowing, is an open item for simulation before the orbit is fixed.

7. Radial transit and radial plumbing

Crew move between bands in a pressurized two-seat car on a rail along each arm, docking to the hub-facing port of each module. A pressurized spoke tube, which is what the station will need, would add about 60 t at roughly 90 kg per metre over three arms and is deferred to the station's own design. The car gives the inter-band transition measurements (AGRE-F-005, F-006) and its transit is a scheduled, trimmed event.

The water and gas lines along the arms are the rotating-frame fluids test bed. Static head from hub to tip is ρω²r²/2 = 1.8 MPa, about 18 atmospheres; the station's rings at 390 m see 1.9 MPa. Pumps at the tip work against it; a line draining toward the hub has it for free. Coriolis asymmetry in radial flow, two-phase behaviour and separator performance at each gravity level are measured on the lines and in the laboratory module (AGRE-F-011).

9. Crew and campaign model

AGRE-F-007 asks for at least six months of exposure per subject and AGRE-F-014 for the study to be powered before it starts. Rev B baselines 180-day campaigns with ten crew: four at 1g, two at Mars gravity, two at lunar gravity, two in the rotating hub. The first campaign is a 60-day shakedown.

14. Open items

  • Coupled attitude simulation. Bounded motion of the spin axis with nodal regression, despun node pointing and array shadowing, before the orbit is fixed.
  • Transfer lock cycle. Spin-up torque on a loaded lock, reaction into the bearing, cycle time and leak budget per cycle.
  • Arm first modes. Stiffness and damping of a 344 m open truss with a moving 2 t car; whether the car's Coriolis load sets the rail design.
  • Module thermal at 1g. Natural convection inside the 1g and partial-g modules changes cabin air handling and condensation behaviour from the ISS design basis.
  • Assembly sequence. Arm erection and module hanging before spin-up, with the robotic and EVA campaign, is costed but not sequenced.
